#### How subnetting works

An IPv4 address has 32 bits. The prefix length — the number after the slash — says how many of them identify the network; the rest number the hosts inside it. A /24 leaves 8 host bits, so 2⁸ = 256 addresses. The first is the **network address** and the last the **broadcast address**, so 254 are usable for devices.

Every extra prefix bit halves the block: a /24 splits into two /25s, four /26s or eight /27s. **VLSM** (variable-length subnet masking) uses different sizes inside one network — a /25 for 100 office PCs, a /30 for a router link — to avoid wasting addresses. IPv6 LANs simply use /64 each; sites usually get a /48 or /56.

### FAQ

**Where does IP Subnet Calculator run?**
IP Subnet Calculator runs in this tab on Toolspire. What you type or drop in is not sent to us and is not stored on a server.

**How many usable hosts does a /24 have?**
254. A /24 has 256 addresses, but the first (network) and last (broadcast) can’t be given to devices. In general, usable hosts = 2^(32 − prefix) − 2, except /31 and /32.

**What is a wildcard mask?**
The inverse of the subnet mask: 0 bits must match and 1 bits can be anything. For 255.255.255.0 it is 0.0.0.255. Cisco access lists and OSPF network statements use wildcard masks.

**How do I find the network and broadcast address?**
The network address is the IP with all host bits set to 0 (a bitwise AND with the mask); the broadcast address has all host bits set to 1. For 192.168.1.10/24 they are 192.168.1.0 and 192.168.1.255.

**Can a /31 be used?**
Yes, on point-to-point links between two routers. RFC 3021 lets both addresses be used because a link with two ends needs no broadcast address.

**What subnet size should I use for an IPv6 LAN?**
A /64. Stateless address autoconfiguration (SLAAC) needs it, and a /64 still holds 18 quintillion addresses. Give each site a /48 or /56 to split into /64s.

**What is VLSM?**
Variable-length subnet masking: dividing one network into subnets of different sizes to match each LAN’s needs. Allocate the largest subnet first so every block stays aligned — the VLSM tab does this for you.

#### What the results mean

| --- | --- | --- |

#### Why your speed may be lower than your plan

- **Wi-Fi**: walls, distance and older routers or phones cut speed. Test with a cable, or close to the router on 5 GHz, to see what the line delivers.
- **Other devices**: TVs, updates and backups on the same connection share the bandwidth during the test.
- **Peak hours**: evenings are busiest, especially on mobile networks and shared fibre.
- **VPNs and proxies** add distance and encryption overhead.
- **Very fast plans**: above roughly 1 Gbps, the browser and device can become the limit rather than the line.

### FAQ

**Where does Internet Speed Test run?**
Internet Speed Test has to reach the network for a public lookup. The page says what leaves your device. Toolspire does not keep a copy of the query.

**How accurate is this speed test?**
It uses the same method as Cloudflare’s own test: precise browser timings, server time removed, and the 90th-percentile speed of many transfers. Results from different test sites differ a little because they use different servers and methods.

**How much data does a speed test use?**
It depends on your speed: a full test uses up to a few hundred MB on a very fast line, less on slower ones. Data saver mode uses at most about 39 MB — the page shows the exact amount after each test.

**What is a good ping for gaming?**
Under 30 ms is excellent and under 60 ms is good for most online games; jitter should stay below about 10–20 ms. Above 100 ms you will notice lag in fast-paced games.

**What is the difference between Mbps and MB/s?**
Speeds are quoted in megabits per second (Mbps); file sizes in megabytes. One byte is 8 bits, so 100 Mbps downloads about 12.5 MB per second.

**Is the speed test private?**
The test connects your browser to Cloudflare’s speed test servers, which see your IP address like any website does. Toolspire doesn’t receive or store your results; your history stays in this tab.

**Why is upload slower than download?**
Most home and mobile plans are asymmetric — designed to receive more than they send. Fibre plans are often symmetric, so similar upload and download speeds are normal there.

2. **Type the link speed in Mbps** — Use the rate you expect to get, not the number on the router box, if you already know the line is slower than the plan.

### Guide

#### Why 100 Mbps is not 100 MB/s

Network speeds are sold in megabits. Files are sized in megabytes. There are eight bits in a byte, so a 100 Mbps plan moves 12.5 decimal megabytes a second when every bit is file data. Dividing a gigabyte by 100, as if the units matched, gives 10 seconds and is wrong by a factor of eight. This page keeps the units apart: you pick MB, GB or TB, you type Mbps, and the 8× happens in the formula. It does not open a socket and it does not time your own line. The speed test is the page that does that.

No. MB, GB and TB on this page are powers of 1,000. A gibibyte (1024³) would take about 7% longer at the same megabit rate.

MAC Address Generator on Toolspire. Check a MAC address, name the unicast and local-admin bits. No account is required.

### At a glance

- MAC Address Generator on Toolspire: A MAC is **48 bits**, shown as six hex bytes. Colons, dashes and Cisco groups of four (`001A.2B3C.4D5E`) all parse.
- The **lowest bit of the first byte** is the group bit. 1 means multicast or broadcast. 0 means unicast.
- The **next bit** is the local bit. 1 means locally administered. 0 means a universally administered, vendor-style address.
- Generate sets those bits to **local and unicast** (`xxxxxx10` in the low two bits). It does not copy a real vendor prefix, and it does not look one up.

### Features

- **Read the two bits that matter**: The detail says unicast or multicast, and local or universal. The other bytes are not turned into a manufacturer name.
- **One canonical spelling**: Whatever separators you paste, the headline is lowercase hex with colons.
- **A lab address, not a disguise**: The button builds a local unicast address for a virtual machine or a lab you are allowed to configure. It is not a way onto someone else’s network.

### How to use the MAC Address Generator

1. **Paste an address to read it** — Colons, dashes or Cisco dots are fine. You need 12 hex digits. Anything else is refused.
2. **Read local versus universal** — A burned-in adapter address has the local bit clear. An address a hypervisor invented usually has it set.
3. **Generate only for a machine you may configure** — Press the button, then use the result in a lab or a VM setting you control. Do not put it on a network where you are not allowed to choose the address.

### Guide

#### Why the generated address is locally administered

The IEEE leaves a bit so that software can mint an address without colliding with a factory prefix. That is the local bit, the second-lowest bit of the first octet. This generator sets it and clears the group bit, so the result is a unicast address that does not pretend to be a particular vendor’s hardware. There is no manufacturer database on the page, because a partial list would mis-name most addresses. Use the result where you are allowed to set a virtual adapter. It is not a tool for slipping past an access list on a network you do not administer.

### FAQ

**Where does MAC Address Generator run?**

**What does locally administered mean?**
The address was chosen by software or by an administrator, not burned into a card by a manufacturer. The second-lowest bit of the first octet is 1.

**What is the multicast bit?**
The lowest bit of the first octet. When it is 1, the address names a group, not a single adapter. ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff is the broadcast address and that bit is set.

**Will this tell me the vendor?**
No. The other five bytes are not looked up. A vendor guess from a short table would be wrong too often to print.

**Can I use a generated address on any network?**
Only on a lab or a virtual machine you are allowed to configure. The page will not help you substitute an address to get past someone else’s filter.

### Guide

#### One abbreviation, eight groups

An IPv6 address is eight 16-bit groups.
