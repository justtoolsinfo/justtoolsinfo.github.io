### Guide

#### What is a good typing speed?

| Speed | Level |
| --- | --- |
| Under 20 WPM | Beginner — focus on accuracy and finger position |
| 20–35 WPM | Learning — enough for everyday use |
| 35–50 WPM | Average — what many office jobs expect |
| 50–70 WPM | Fast |
| 70 WPM and above | Very fast — professional typists and transcribers |

#### How the scores are worked out

- **WPM** = characters of correctly typed words (with their spaces) ÷ 5 ÷ minutes.
- **Raw speed** counts everything you typed, mistakes included.
- **Accuracy** = keys pressed correctly ÷ all keys pressed.
- **Key depressions per hour** = characters typed per minute × 60 — the measure many Indian recruitment typing tests quote.
- **Exam practice**: full mistakes are left-out, extra or wrong words; half mistakes are wrong capitals, punctuation, or words joined or split. Error rate = (full + half ÷ 2) ÷ words reached × 100.

### Guide

#### How the seats are filled

Seats are numbered along a path — down each column of seats, or along each row — and filled in that order. At every seat the planner takes the next student from the group that clashes least with the neighbours already seated, and prefers the group with the most students still waiting, so the groups run out together.

Clashes are ranked: two classmates **on the same desk** are avoided first, then **side by side across an aisle**, then **front and back**, then **diagonally**. Within a group, students keep the order you entered, so roll numbers still run in sequence along the path and are easy to find.

A second pass swaps seats where that removes a clash that was left over. If a clash cannot be avoided — for example when one class is more than half the room — the planner tells you how many pairs remain instead of hiding them.

#### Which rule to choose

| Rule | Who is kept apart | Groups needed | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Side by side only | Desk-mates and side neighbours | 2 | Each column is one class — easy for handing out papers |
| Side by side and front–back | The four nearest seats | 2 | A checkerboard; the usual choice |
| All around, diagonals too | All eight surrounding seats | 4 | The strictest plan, for high-stakes exams |
| Don’t mix | Nobody | 1 | One class after another |

#### How many students fit in a room

Seats = rows × desks across × seats per desk. Take away any seats you mark as not in use.

| Rows | Desks across | Seats per desk | Seats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 4 | 2 | 40 |
| 6 | 5 | 2 | 60 |
| 8 | 5 | 2 | 80 |
| 10 | 6 | 2 | 120 |
| 6 | 5 | 1 | 30 |
| 6 | 4 | 3 | 72 |

- **Notice-board list** — for each class, which roll numbers go to which room, so students find their room at a glance.

#### Tips for a fair seating plan

- Seat at least two classes or subjects in every session; a single class can only be spaced out, not mixed.
- For a clean checkerboard no group should be more than half the seats in use. If one is, add a room so that spare seats can act as gaps.
- Keep a few spare seats in every room for late changes, and mark broken desks before printing.
- Use **Shuffled** order for class tests where friends would otherwise sit in roll-number order near each other.
- Print the notice-board list the day before, and the charts and attendance sheets on the day.

### How to balance a chemical equation

| You type | Balanced |
| --- | --- |
| `H2 + O2 -> H2O` | 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O |
| `Fe + O2 -> Fe2O3` | 4 Fe + 3 O₂ → 2 Fe₂O₃ |

| `KMnO4 + HCl -> KCl + MnCl2 + H2O + Cl2` | 2 KMnO₄ + 16 HCl → 2 KCl + 2 MnCl₂ + 8 H₂O + 5 Cl₂ |
| `Cr2O7^2- + H+ + e- -> Cr^3+ + H2O` | Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14 H⁺ + 6 e⁻ → 2 Cr³⁺ + 7 H₂O |

#### How the balancing works

A balanced equation has the same number of atoms of every element on both sides, and the same total charge. Each element gives one equation in the unknown coefficients: for hydrogen in H₂ + O₂ → H₂O, twice the coefficient of H₂ must equal twice the coefficient of H₂O.

The tool solves all of those equations together, exactly, and scales the answer to the smallest whole numbers. If there is no solution, the equation cannot be balanced as written — usually a formula is wrong or a substance is missing. If there is more than one independent solution, the equation is really two reactions written as one (for example C + O₂ → CO + CO₂), and the tool tells you so.

#### Digits before a + or − sign

Plain text cannot tell a charge from a subscript: `Fe3+` should be Fe³⁺ but `I3-` is I₃⁻. The tool reads a digit before the sign as the charge on a single atom (`Fe3+`, `Cu2+`, `O2-`) and as a subscript in anything larger (`MnO4-`, `NH4+`); two digits are split (`SO42-` is SO₄²⁻). If that reading cannot be balanced it tries the other, and it always shows how each ion was read. Write `Fe^3+` or `I3^-` to leave no doubt.

### Guide

#### The three rules

- **Aufbau principle** — electrons fill the lowest-energy subshell that has room, in the order 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p.

- **Hund’s rule** — within a subshell, every orbital gets one electron before any orbital gets a second.

#### Elements that break the filling order

Twenty elements have a ground state the filling order does not predict: Cr, Cu, Nb, Mo, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, La, Ce, Gd, Pt, Au, Ac, Th, Pa, U, Np, Cm and Lr. In most of them an electron moves from the s subshell into d, because a half-filled or filled d subshell is slightly more stable — chromium is 3d⁵ 4s¹ rather than 3d⁴ 4s², and copper 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ rather than 3d⁹ 4s².

These cannot be worked out from the rule; they are measured. The tool shows the measured configuration and, beside it, what the rule alone would give.

#### Why ions lose 4s before 3d

In a neutral atom 4s fills before 3d. Once 3d holds electrons, though, it lies lower in energy than 4s, so when a transition metal becomes a positive ion the 4s electrons leave first. Iron, [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s², becomes Fe²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁶ and Fe³⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁵. Writing Fe²⁺ as [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² — the configuration of a neutral atom with 24 electrons — is the commonest mistake, and Practice mode points it out.

### Guide

#### The half-life formulas

| To find | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Amount left | `N = N₀ × (½)^(t ÷ T½)` |
| Starting amount | `N₀ = N × 2^(t ÷ T½)` |
| Time passed | `t = T½ × log₂(N₀ ÷ N)` |
| Half-life | `T½ = t × ln 2 ÷ ln(N₀ ÷ N)` |
| Decay constant | `λ = ln 2 ÷ T½ ≈ 0.693 ÷ T½` |

#### A worked example

Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8.0249 days. How much of a 400 MBq dose is left after 24 days? The time is 24 ÷ 8.0249 = 2.991 half-lives, so the amount left is 400 × (½)^2.991 = 50.3 MBq — just over an eighth, because three half-lives would take 24.07 days.

And how long until only 1 % is left? t = 8.0249 × log₂(100) = 8.0249 × 6.644 = 53.3 days. A rule of thumb follows from this: after 7 half-lives less than 1 % remains, and after 10 less than 0.1 %.

**Where does Half-Life Calculator run?**

**What is a half-life?**
The time it takes for half of the atoms in a radioactive sample to decay. It never changes: whether you start with a gram or a tonne, and whatever has already decayed, half of what is left decays in each half-life.

**How do I calculate the half-life from two measurements?**
Use `T½ = t × ln 2 ÷ ln(N₀ ÷ N)`. For example, if a count rate falls from 800 to 100 in 15 hours, N₀ ÷ N = 8 = 2³, so three half-lives passed and the half-life is 5 hours. Choose **Half-life** in the calculator to get the working.

**What is the difference between half-life, decay constant and mean life?**
They describe the same thing. The decay constant λ is the chance per unit time that one atom decays; the half-life is ln 2 ÷ λ; the mean life — the average time an atom survives — is 1 ÷ λ, about 1.443 half-lives. The calculator accepts any of the three.

**Why do radiocarbon labs use 5,568 years instead of 5,700?**
Radiocarbon dating began with Willard Libby’s value of 5,568 years. To keep every published result comparable, laboratories still report conventional radiocarbon ages with it (an international convention since 1977) and then convert them to calendar years with a calibration curve such as IntCal20, which also corrects for the true half-life and the changing amount of carbon-14 in the air.

**Does a sample ever decay completely?**
In theory the amount only halves, so it never reaches zero; in practice a sample is effectively gone after about 50 half-lives, when fewer than one atom in a thousand million million is left. With a small number of atoms, the last ones decay at random times.

**Can I use this for medicines, caffeine or other exponential decay?**
Yes — the formula is the same for anything that halves at a steady rate, such as how a drug is cleared from the body. Leave the isotope empty and type the half-life. Medicine half-lives vary from person to person, so ask a doctor or pharmacist about your own dose.

**Where do the half-lives come from?**
From NUBASE2020 (Kondev and others, Chinese Physics C, 2021), the evaluation of nuclear properties published by the IAEA’s Atomic Mass Data Center. It uses 1 year = 365.2422 days, and so does this calculator.

### Guide

#### Why decay is random but predictable

A radioactive nucleus has a fixed chance of decaying in any second, the decay constant λ, and nothing — not its age, temperature or neighbours — changes it. Which atom goes next is pure chance. The smaller the sample, the larger the wobble. The typical difference from the expected number is √(N₀ × p × (1 − p)), where p is the fraction expected to be left — about 16 atoms out of 1,000 after one half-life, but only 1.6 % of the sample instead of 5 % with ten times fewer atoms.

### Guide
